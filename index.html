Mastering Equivalent Depth of Field & Aperture

How to Calculate Equivalent Depth of Field

To achieve the exact same field of view and depth of field (background blur/bokeh) on a crop sensor camera compared to a standard 35mm Full Frame camera, you must multiply both the focal length and the aperture f-number by the crop factor:

Equivalent Focal Length: f_FF = f_crop × Crop Factor
Equivalent Aperture (DoF): N_FF = N_crop × Crop Factor

For example, on a 1.5x APS-C camera, an f/1.8 lens behaves optically with the depth of field of an f/2.7 lens on Full Frame ($1.8 \times 1.5 = 2.7$).

The Physical Optics Reason: Entrance Pupil Diameter

Why does multiplying the aperture by the crop factor produce identical depth of field? Depth of field and background blur at infinity depend directly on the physical diameter of the lens entrance pupil ($D = f / N$).

Consider a 50mm f/1.8 on APS-C: $$D = \frac{50\text{mm}}{1.8} = 27.8\text{mm}$$ Now consider its Full Frame equivalent (75mm f/2.7): $$D = \frac{75\text{mm}}{2.7} = 27.8\text{mm}$$ Both lenses have the exact same 27.8mm physical aperture diameter. When framed identically from the same distance, the light cones, blur circles, and depth of field are indistinguishable!

Depth of Field vs. Exposure: The Great Misconception

Photographers often ask: "Does an f/1.8 lens become darker like an f/2.7 lens on APS-C?"

No! Exposure brightness remains unchanged. F-number is the ratio of focal length to aperture diameter ($f / D$), which dictates the concentration of light per unit area (photons per $\text{mm}^2$).

  • For Exposure / Light Intensity: f/1.8 is always f/1.8 regardless of sensor size. A 1/125s shutter at ISO 100 on an f/1.8 lens yields identical image brightness on APS-C, Full Frame, and Medium Format.
  • For Depth of Field & Bokeh: The smaller sensor requires shorter focal lengths for the same framing, resulting in a deeper in-focus zone equivalent to $f/1.8 \times 1.5 = \text{f/}2.7$.
  • Total Light Collected: Because a Full Frame sensor has 2.25x more surface area, it gathers 2.25x more total photons across the entire frame at the same f-number, giving Full Frame roughly a 1.1-stop signal-to-noise advantage.
